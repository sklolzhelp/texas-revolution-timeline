Texas Revolution Timeline

1826 – 1836 | A Visual Journey to Independence

Rising Tensions: Fredonian Rebellion

1826–1827

Empresario Haden Edwards tried to secede from Mexico after a land grant dispute. The rebellion failed, but it marked the first serious talk of Texas separating from Mexico.

Mier y Terán Report

1828–1829

General Mier y Terán reported that most people in East Texas were from the U.S., did not speak Spanish, and did not practice Catholicism. He recommended more Mexican troops, immigration, and trade — leading to the Law of April 6, 1830.

Law of April 6, 1830

April 6, 1830

Banned U.S. immigration to Texas, made bringing slaves illegal, and imposed tariffs. Settlers who depended on trade and family immigration were furious.

Trouble at Anahuac & Turtle Bayou Resolutions

1832

Tensions over tariffs led to arrests including William B. Travis. Texans wrote the Turtle Bayou Resolutions pledging loyalty to Santa Anna, Mexico, and the Constitution of 1824.

Santa Anna Becomes Dictator

1833

Elected as a federalist, Santa Anna later switched to centralism, concentrating power in Mexico City and becoming a dictator. This destroyed hopes of shared power.

Arrest of Stephen F. Austin

1833–1834

Austin suggested Texas become its own state. He was arrested for treason and jailed ~8 months. After release, he concluded reconciliation with Santa Anna was impossible.

Battle of Gonzales — “Come and Take It”

October 2, 1835

Mexico tried to reclaim a cannon loaned to Gonzales. Texans raised the famous “Come and Take It” flag and fired the first shots of the Revolution. This was the opening battle.

Siege of the Alamo

February 23 – March 6, 1836

Santa Anna surrounded the Alamo for 13 days. Nearly all defenders were killed. The delay gave the Texas government time to organize. “Remember the Alamo!” became the ultimate rallying cry.

Travis’s Letter — “Victory or Death”

February 24, 1836

William B. Travis wrote to “All Americans of the World,” asking for reinforcements, vowing never to surrender, and signing “Victory or Death.” The letter inspired patriotism across Texas and the U.S.

Defenders of the Alamo

Travis, James Bowie, David Crockett, and Tejanos defended the mission. Survivor Susanna Dickinson carried the news of the fall. Their sacrifice became a powerful symbol of the fight for independence.

Convention of 1836 & Declaration of Independence

March 1836 — Washington-on-the-Brazos

Delegates signed the Texas Declaration of Independence (authored by George Childress) and named Sam Houston commander of the army. This created the Republic of Texas with an interim government. Lorenzo de Zavala helped write the first Constitution and became the first vice president.

Goliad Massacre

March 1836

After surrendering at the Battle of Coleto Creek, James Fannin and about 340 prisoners were executed at Goliad. “Remember Goliad!” became another battle cry that strengthened Texan resolve.

Battle of San Jacinto

April 21, 1836

Sam Houston surprised Santa Anna’s camp. The battle lasted only 18 minutes. Santa Anna was captured. This decisive last battle secured Texas independence. Juan Seguín led the Tejano unit and had earlier carried a message for help from the Alamo.

Treaties of Velasco

May 14, 1836

Santa Anna agreed to withdraw Mexican troops and to support Texas independence with a border at the Rio Grande. The Mexican Congress later rejected the border terms, but the war was effectively over.

Why 1836 Matters

In 1836, Texas gained its independence from Mexico and became the Republic of Texas. The sacrifices at the Alamo and Goliad, and the victory at San Jacinto, created a new nation that would later join the United States.

Key People of the Revolution

George Childress
Author of the Texas Declaration of Independence
Lorenzo de Zavala
Helped write the first Texas Constitution; first vice president
James Fannin
Captured at Coleto Creek and executed at Goliad
Sam Houston
Commander of the Texan Army; defeated Santa Anna at San Jacinto
Santa Anna
Dictator of Mexico; led the siege of the Alamo; signed the Treaties of Velasco
William B. Travis
Co-commander of the Alamo; wrote the “Victory or Death” letter
Juan Seguín
Carried message from the Alamo; led Tejano unit at San Jacinto
